Start With the Basics: Thermostat and Temperature Settings

Before you assume something is broken, verify that your oven is actually set to the temperature you think it is. Ovens have a thermostat dial or digital display, and it's easy to accidentally turn it to the "off" position or set it way too low. Check that the temperature control is positioned where you intended it to be.

Next, make sure the oven mode is correct. Many ovens have separate settings for baking, broiling, convection, and self-cleaning. If you've selected broil by mistake, your standard heating element won't activate. Flip through the settings on your display or dial to confirm you're in the right mode for what you're cooking.

Give the oven 15 to 20 minutes to preheat. Modern ovens signal when they've reached temperature, but that signal doesn't mean it's at full heat. The elements inside still need time to reach the target temperature you've set. If you're testing quickly, you might mistake a slow startup for a complete failure.

Listen and Feel for the Heating Element

Once the oven has been set and running for a few minutes, get close and listen. You should hear a subtle hum or soft buzzing sound from the element as it powers up. This is the sound of electricity flowing through the heating coil. If you hear absolutely nothing—no hum, no crackle, no electrical activity—that's a red flag.

If the oven is safe to touch on the exterior (and only after confirming it's been running for a minute or two), place your hand near the oven door or window and feel for warmth radiating from inside. You should detect heat within 3 to 5 minutes. If the oven feels completely cold after 10 minutes of running, the heating element likely isn't firing.

For electric ovens, the heating element is typically a metal coil you can see if you open the oven door. Look for visible damage: blackening, cracks, or a coil that appears disconnected. For gas ovens, you won't see a coil—instead, look at the burner itself (a U-shaped tube at the oven's base). A gas flame should ignite when the oven runs. No visible spark or flame means the igniter or gas line may be the problem.

Check for Error Codes on Your Display

Most ovens built in the last 10 to 15 years have digital displays that show error codes when something goes wrong. These codes are your oven's way of communicating the exact problem. Common heating-related codes include "E2" or "F2" (which often signal a temperature sensor issue), "E5" or "F5" (heating element failure), or codes specific to your brand.

Write down any code you see. Search your oven's model number (usually on a label inside the oven or in your manual) plus the error code. You'll find what that code means and whether it's something you can address or something that requires a technician. Most manufacturers publish their code guides online for free.

If no code appears but the oven still won't heat, that tells you the oven's diagnostic system isn't detecting a problem—which could mean the issue is intermittent, or the sensor itself is malfunctioning. Either way, professional diagnosis becomes important at this stage.

Check Your Power and Circuit Breaker

Electric ovens are power-hungry appliances. If your circuit breaker has tripped, the oven won't heat no matter what else is working. Head to your electrical panel (usually in a basement, garage, or utility room) and look for any breaker switches in the "off" position or one that's halfway between on and off. Flip any suspect breaker all the way off, then back on.

If the breaker immediately trips again after you turn it back on, you have an electrical fault. Do not keep trying to reset it. This is a safety issue that requires a licensed electrician or appliance repair professional.

For gas ovens, check that the oven still has power to its display and controls. If the display is dark or unresponsive, the control board may have lost power. Look at nearby outlets or the breaker panel. A gas oven still needs electricity to run the igniter and controls, even though the flame itself is gas-powered.

When DIY Stops: Signs You Need a Professional

Once you've checked the thermostat, listened for the element, looked for error codes, and verified your power supply, you've done what a homeowner safely can. Anything beyond this point involves electrical components, gas lines, or parts that require professional tools and certifications to replace.

You need to call a technician if the heating element appears visibly damaged (cracked, severely blackened, or disconnected). You also need help if the oven shows an error code related to the heating element or temperature sensor. If the breaker keeps tripping when the oven runs, that's an electrical fault that's beyond a safe DIY fix.

Similarly, if you smell gas when the oven runs (a faint rotten-egg scent that the utility company adds to natural gas for safety), shut off the oven immediately and call a professional. Gas leaks are a serious hazard. Never try to troubleshoot this on your own.

Can I replace the heating element myself?

Technically, yes—the heating element on many electric ovens unbolts from the back or bottom of the oven box. However, you must disconnect the oven from power first (at the breaker), and you need the correct replacement part for your exact model. Most homeowners find it simpler to hire a technician, especially since a misaligned or incorrectly installed element can cause bigger problems down the road.

Why is my oven heating unevenly even though it's working?

Uneven heating is usually caused by a worn thermostat or a malfunctioning convection fan (if your oven has one). It can also result from items blocking air vents inside the oven box. Check that your oven racks aren't touching the walls and that nothing is stored inside. If the problem persists, the thermostat or fan may need replacement by a professional.

What should I do if my oven won't heat and smells like gas?

Stop using the oven immediately and call a professional right away. Never try to fix a gas smell on your own. A leak or faulty igniter can create a serious safety hazard. Get it inspected and repaired by a certified technician before using it again.
