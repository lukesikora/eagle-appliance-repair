Why Washers Leak and How to Spot the Real Problem

Water pooling under or around your washer signals one of four issues: a punctured hose, a failed door seal, an inlet valve leak, or a cracked drum.

Check the hose first. Unplug the machine, pull it forward, and inspect the inlet and drain hoses for cracks, kinks, or loose connections. Hose replacement costs $80–$150 and fixes roughly 30% of leaks. A faulty door gasket (rubber seal) costs $120–$180 to replace and shows itself as water leaking from the door during the wash cycle.

Inlet valve leaks develop slowly—you'll notice water trickling from the back or sides before the machine fills. This part runs $100–$160 to replace. Drum leaks are the most serious. The drum itself doesn't crack easily, but the seal that keeps water inside fails. This repair costs $280–$350 and requires partial disassembly.

Drum, Agitator, and Spin Cycle Problems in Riverton Homes

Your washer drum should spin freely. If it's grinding, squeaking, or not spinning at all, the bearing, pulley, or belt is failing.

Squeaking usually means a worn drum bearing. This bearing wears faster in homes with hard water—and Riverton's municipal water is moderately hard, especially in areas served by the South Jordan water system. That mineral buildup accelerates bearing wear. Replacement costs $220–$320.

A drum that won't spin but fills normally points to a broken belt or a transmission problem. Belt replacement is $140–$200. Transmission failure is rarer but costs $350–$500 and might push some homeowners toward replacement rather than repair.

Agitator problems (the post in the center that moves clothes) show up as clothes not getting cleaned or the agitator moving loosely. A replacement agitator costs $90–$160.

Common Mistakes That Make Washer Problems Worse

Ignoring small leaks is the biggest mistake. A drip under the machine that starts as a few drops becomes 20 gallons by next week. Water damage to flooring and subflooring gets expensive fast. Call for repair as soon as you see moisture.

Overloading the drum also damages bearings and seals prematurely. Modern washers have weight limits—typically 12–16 pounds depending on the model. Cramming in 20+ pounds of laundry strains the drum rotation and weakens seals.

Using too much detergent doesn't clean better and leaves residue that blocks drain lines. High-efficiency washers especially need lower soap doses. This buildup is a common reason washers don't drain properly.

When to Repair vs. Replace Your Washing Machine

A repair makes sense if your washer is under 8 years old and the fix costs less than 50% of a replacement. A new mid-range washer runs $600–$1200. If your repair is $300, fixing the old machine is smart.

Replace the machine if it's 10+ years old, the repair exceeds $400, or it has multiple failing parts (e.g., failing drum bearing plus leaking seal plus broken belt). Older washers also consume more water and energy, so replacement saves money long-term.

Most Riverton homes have washers between 6–12 years old. In that range, most repairs are worth doing.
