Why Your Washing Machine Drum Won't Spin

A drum that won't turn signals one of three mechanical or electrical failures. The machine may fill with water, drain, and complete its cycle, but the drum itself stays locked in place. This tells you the issue isn't with water supply or drainage—it's in the drive system.

The spin cycle relies on a motor, drum bearing, pulley system, and control board all working in concert. When any one of these fails, the drum halts. Understanding which component is broken determines both your repair cost and how quickly we can get your laundry running again.

Drum Bearing Failure: The Most Common Culprit

The drum bearing is a metal ring that lets the drum rotate smoothly inside the outer tub. After years of use—especially in households doing 8–10 loads per week—this bearing wears down from friction and moisture exposure. When it fails, the drum may feel stiff, make grinding noises, or stop spinning altogether.

Replacing a drum bearing runs $200–$400 in labor and parts for most standard models. The job involves removing the drum assembly, which takes 45 minutes to 90 minutes depending on your machine's design. We see this failure constantly in West Jordan homes built in the 2000s and early 2010s, where original washers are now hitting their 12–15 year lifespan.

If your machine is under 8 years old and the drum seized suddenly, a bearing failure is still possible but less likely than a motor or control issue. If it's older and the drum has been making creaking or grinding sounds for the past few months, the bearing is almost certainly your problem.

Motor Failure and Drive Coupling Issues

The drive motor powers the drum's rotation during the spin cycle. A dead or failing motor means no power reaches the drum at all. You'll often notice the motor not humming when the spin cycle starts, or the drum wobbles loosely without any rotational force.

Motor replacement costs $300–$600 in parts and labor. Some washers use a drive coupling—a plastic connector between the motor and drum—that burns out before the motor itself fails. Coupling replacement is cheaper (around $150–$250) and faster (30–45 minutes), but motor replacement takes longer because the entire assembly may need removal.

In West Jordan's dry climate, motor failures sometimes accelerate because lint and dust can accumulate inside the housing more readily. If your unit is venting lint around the seal or you notice a burning smell during the spin cycle, the motor is likely failing.

Control Board Malfunction

The control board tells the motor when to engage and how fast to spin. A faulty board can prevent the spin cycle from starting even though the motor itself is fine. You might hear the machine drain but see no drum movement, with no error code displayed—or a cryptic code you can't decipher.

Control board replacement ranges from $150–$500 depending on your machine's complexity and board availability. Some modern boards are proprietary and take a few days to order, while others are standard components stocked locally and can be installed same-day. Diagnosing a control board issue requires testing the motor and bearings first, so never assume it's the board without ruling out mechanical problems.

How to Tell Which Component Has Failed

Listen closely before calling for service. A grinding or squealing sound during the spin cycle points to bearing wear. A dead silence when spin should start, with no humming from the motor area, suggests motor or coupling failure. If the machine drains, fills, and completes other cycles normally but refuses to spin, a control board fault is more likely.

Check the error code if your machine displays one. Look it up in your manual—it often narrows the culprit significantly. Feel the drum by hand (with power off): if it won't turn by hand and feels completely stuck, a bearing is likely seized. If it turns freely but the motor won't engage, the motor or board is the issue.

Don't run repeated spin cycles trying to diagnose the problem yourself. Doing so can damage a failing bearing further or cause the motor to overheat, which converts a $300 repair into a $600 one.

Common Mistakes That Raise Your Repair Cost

Ignoring strange noises for weeks before calling a technician is the biggest mistake we see. A bearing that's grinding will eventually seize completely and possibly damage the drum or tub, turning a $300 repair into a $500+ job.

Running the machine continuously on short cycles after it starts malfunctioning can overheat the motor. This destroys windings inside and makes the motor unrepairable—you'll need a full replacement instead of troubleshooting a different component.

Attempting DIY bearing replacement without proper tools usually results in stripped bolt threads or a misaligned drum, both of which require professional correction and add $100–$200 to your final bill.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a washing machine spin cycle repair take?

Most repairs finish within 2–3 hours. Bearing and motor jobs typically take 1–2 hours depending on your machine's design. Control board replacements can be done in under an hour if the board is in stock; if we need to order it, installation waits for delivery, usually 2–5 business days.

Is it worth repairing a 10-year-old washing machine?

Yes, usually. A $300 bearing repair extends a $600–$800 machine's life by another 3–5 years. If the repair cost exceeds 50% of the machine's replacement value and it's already 12+ years old, replacement becomes the smarter choice. We'll give you an honest assessment during diagnosis.

Can I prevent washing machine bearing failure?

Not completely, but you can slow it down. Don't overload the drum, use the correct detergent amount, and avoid letting wet clothes sit in the drum for hours after the cycle ends. Hard water accelerates bearing wear, so a water softener or descaling tablets help. Regular maintenance adds years to bearing life.

Why does my spin cycle start but the drum doesn't move?

This usually means the motor is running but the drum isn't turning—a classic sign of a broken coupling, worn pulley, or seized bearing. It could also be a belt slipping. Have it diagnosed before running the cycle again, or you risk burning out the motor.
